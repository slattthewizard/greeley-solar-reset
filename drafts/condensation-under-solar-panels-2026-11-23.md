# Condensation Under Solar Panels: Leak or Just Cold Weather?

You spot water on the roof under your array, or a damp patch in the attic, and you wonder whether it's a leak or just condensation under solar panels. In Northern Colorado, with our freeze-thaw swings, it's a fair question. Sometimes the water is perfectly normal. Sometimes it isn't.

We won't redo the full diagnosis here. Our page on [finding a roof leak under solar panels](/blog/roof-leak-under-solar-panels/) covers that, and [do solar panels cause roof leaks](/blog/do-solar-panels-cause-roof-leaks/) covers whether the array itself is to blame. This page deals with one narrow thing: telling harmless moisture from a real leak.

## What's normal: moisture that isn't coming through the roof

A solar array changes how a roof sheds water and snow. Several things can leave water or damp spots that look scary and aren't a roof failure.

**Frost on the back of panels.** On a clear, cold night, panel glass loses heat fast. Frost or dew forms on the surfaces, and the underside of panels near the roof can get damp as temperatures swing. When the sun hits, it melts and drips. That's moisture from the air, not from a hole.

**Snow melting and running under the array.** Panels shed snow in sheets. Meltwater can run down the shingles beneath the array and collect at the lower edge. It looks like a lot of water, and it's just the roof doing its job. Our post on [snow sliding off solar panels](/blog/snow-sliding-off-solar-panels/) covers what happens when that snow lets go.

**Ice at the eaves.** A line of ice at the bottom edge of the roof, below the array, is common in Colorado winters. Heat leaving the house warms the roof deck, snow melts, and the water refreezes at the cold eave. The array can change where snow gathers and melts, which sometimes makes this more visible. If ice builds up and the water backs under shingles, you can get a leak, but that's an ice dam issue, not a mount failure.

**Condensation in the attic.** Warm, humid air from the house can reach cold roof decking and condense into droplets or frost on the underside. This happens on roofs with no solar at all. It tends to show up as widespread dampness or frost on nail tips across the attic, not a single wet spot.

None of these needs a detach. They may point to attic ventilation or air leakage, which is a conversation for a roofer or insulation contractor.

## What's not normal: a leak at a mount

A mount penetration leak is different. Every rail attaches to the roof through a bracket and flashing, and each one is a small hole that has to stay sealed for decades. When that seal fails, water follows the fastener into the decking.

Signs point toward a real leak when:

- The water shows up in **specific spots** that line up with where mounts sit, not across the whole attic.
- Staining is **repeated** and gets larger over time.
- You see **dark rings, rust on nail tips, or soft wood** right at a penetration.
- It happens **after rain**, even mild rain on a warm day, not just on cold mornings.

A mount leak will keep showing up whatever the temperature is. If it only happens during freezing weather, think moisture. If it follows rainstorms, think leak.

## How to tell the difference

You don't need special tools. A few simple checks sort most cases.

### Look at the timing

Write down when you notice the water. Does it show up on cold, clear mornings and fade by afternoon? That's frost and condensation. Does it appear a few hours after rain or snow melt and linger? That points toward water getting in.

Cold mornings, dry roof by noon: usually moisture. Wet after storms, even on mild days: more suspicious.

### Look for dry-weather stains

After several dry days, check the ceiling and attic again. A stain that's still damp or still growing when nothing has fallen from the sky is either a slow, ongoing leak or an indoor moisture issue. A stain that dried out and stayed the same size is usually old.

### Check the attic at the mount locations

With a flashlight, go to the part of the attic under the array. Look at the underside of the decking where mounts attach. Wet or stained wood right at those points is a red flag. Dry wood with a general frosty sheen over everything is likely condensation.

Take photos with the date, and check the same spots after the next storm and after the next cold snap. Patterns tell you more than a single look.

### Check for ice build-up

From the ground, look at the lower roof edge. A heavy rim of ice, long icicles, or water staining on the soffit under the array can mean an ice dam. That's a roofing and attic issue to take to a roofer.

## What condensation can turn into

Harmless moisture isn't always harmless forever. Repeated condensation in a poorly vented attic can leave decking damp enough to stain, grow mold, or rot. That doesn't mean the array did it, but it does mean the attic needs attention.

Likewise, a small flashing failure can look like condensation for a season before it shows its true colors. If you keep seeing damp wood at mount points, don't wait for it to get worse. Water that sits on decking turns a small repair into a bigger one.

## Where to look first on your own roof

Start with what you can see safely from the ground and from inside. From outside, look along the lower edge of the array for staining on the fascia or soffit, and for a ridge of ice that stays after the rest of the snow is gone. Inside, check the ceilings directly below the array and the corners of the rooms under it.

Don't climb onto a roof with panels on it. Glass is slick when wet or frosty, and a fall or a stepped-on panel is a much bigger problem than a damp spot. If you need eyes on the roof, a roofer or a drone photo is safer than a ladder trip.

Keep a short log: the date, the weather the night before, whether it had rained or snowed, and where the water showed up. Two or three entries are enough to start seeing whether cold mornings or storms are the trigger. That log is also useful to hand a roofer, because it saves them guessing.

## Why Northern Colorado makes this confusing

We get big temperature swings. A winter morning can start well below freezing and be shirt-sleeve weather by afternoon. Snow arrives, melts, refreezes, and slides. That freeze-thaw cycle moves water around a roof in ways that can fool you.

It also means a failing seal gets tested often. A mount that holds in July may weep in March, when ice and meltwater are pushing into every gap. So if you see water only in winter, don't write it off completely. Check the same spots again in spring rain. If they stay dry, you've probably got condensation. If they get wet, you've got your answer.

## FAQ

### Is water under my solar panels always a leak?

No. Frost melting off the panel backs, snow melt running under the array, and condensation in a cold attic can all leave water that looks like a leak. A real leak at a mount tends to repeat after rain, stain the decking at specific attachment points, and keep growing in dry weather.

### Can solar panels cause condensation in the attic?

The panels themselves don't create attic moisture. Condensation forms when warm indoor air meets cold roof decking, and that happens on roofs without solar too. An array can change how snow sits and melts, which may make ice at the eaves more noticeable. Attic ventilation and air sealing are the usual fixes, and that's a job for a roofer.

### How do I check the attic for a mount leak?

Use a flashlight and look at the underside of the decking where the mounts attach. Wet or stained wood, rust on nail tips, or dark rings at those spots suggest a leak. A general frosty or damp sheen across the whole attic points more toward condensation. Take dated photos and compare after storms and after cold snaps.

### Does it matter if the water only shows up in winter?

It helps narrow it down. Water that appears on cold mornings and dries by afternoon is usually frost or condensation. But freeze-thaw cycles put stress on mount seals, so recheck the same spots in spring rain. If they stay dry, you likely have moisture. If they get wet, a flashing has probably failed.
