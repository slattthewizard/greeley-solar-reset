# Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Solar Panels? What to Ask Your Carrier

Most homeowners policies do cover solar panels in some way, but the details depend on how the system is mounted, who owns it, and what your policy says. If you're asking whether homeowners insurance covers solar panels, the short version is that roof-mounted panels are usually treated as part of the house. The long version has a few catches worth knowing before a storm, and not after.

We're a solar detach and reset contractor, and we're not insurance advisers. This page is a list of good questions to ask, not a ruling on your policy. Your policy and your carrier are the source of truth.

One note on scope. Whether insurance pays for taking panels off and putting them back during a roof replacement is its own topic, and we cover it in [does insurance cover solar detach and reset](/blog/does-insurance-cover-solar-detach-reset/). This page is about the broader question of how solar fits into your homeowners coverage in general.

## Roof-mounted panels: usually part of the dwelling

When panels are attached to the roof of your house, insurers typically treat them as part of the dwelling, in the same way they'd treat the roof, the siding, or a built-in water heater. That means the coverage that protects your house generally extends to the array.

"Usually" matters there. It's a general pattern and not a promise, and policies differ. Some carriers treat solar as an attached improvement, and some have specific language about it or ask you to report it.

Check the limit too. If your dwelling coverage was set years ago, before the system went on, the number may not reflect what it would cost to replace the house with the array on it. A solar system adds real replacement cost, and that can push a rebuild past your old limit.

## Tell your carrier you have solar

This is the simplest step on the list. When you add solar, tell your insurance company. If you bought the house with solar already on it, tell them then too.

The reason is straightforward. Coverage is set based on what the carrier knows about the property. If the system was never reported, the limit may not account for it, and you don't want to find that out during a claim. A phone call or a quick note to your agent can fix it.

Ask these questions directly:

- Is my solar system covered as part of the dwelling?
- Does my dwelling limit reflect the cost of the system?
- Is there a separate deductible or a sublimit that applies to solar?
- What documents do you need for the system, such as the install invoice or the permit?

Write down the answers and who gave them.

## Leased panels: usually the leasing company's problem

If your panels are leased, or you pay under a power purchase agreement, you don't own them. The leasing company typically owns the equipment and is usually responsible for insuring it, and the agreement tends to spell out who does what.

That doesn't mean you can ignore it. You're often responsible for the roof underneath, for notifying the company about damage, and for any harm caused to the equipment. Read the lease and call the company. Our guide to [leased solar panels and roof replacement](/blog/leased-solar-panels-roof-replacement/) explains how the company controls removal and reset.

Your homeowners carrier may still want to know the panels are there, and it can affect liability questions on the roof.

## Ground mounts may fall under other structures

If your array sits on the ground, not on the roof, the policy may classify it differently. A freestanding ground mount can fall under other structures, the section that covers things like detached garages, fences, and sheds. That section often has its own limit, usually a percentage of the dwelling coverage, and sometimes different terms.

The same goes for arrays on a barn, shop, or detached building. A solar system on a roof of a detached structure may be covered under that structure's terms.

If you have acreage and a ground array, check that limit against the cost of the system. Our post on [ground mount solar hail damage](/blog/ground-mount-solar-hail-damage/) covers what to inspect after a storm and why classification matters.

## What's typically covered: storms and sudden damage

Homeowners policies generally cover sudden physical loss from named or listed perils, and the ones that matter for solar are hail and wind. In Weld County, hail is the big one, since this is one of the most hail-prone parts of the country. A hailstorm that damages your roof and your panels is the standard claim scenario.

Panels sold in the US are typically certified to IEC 61215 and UL 61730, whose hail test uses 25 mm (1 inch) ice balls at about 23 m/s, roughly 51 mph. That's a baseline and not a guarantee, and larger stones can still crack glass. Our post on [signs of hail damage on solar panels](/blog/signs-of-hail-damage-solar-panels/) shows what to look for.

Falling tree limbs, wind, and other sudden events are commonly covered perils too, depending on your policy. Read the list of covered perils in your own document.

## What's typically not covered: wear and slow decline

Insurance generally isn't built for gradual change. Normal wear, aging, manufacturing defects, and slow output loss over time usually aren't covered as claims. Panels lose a little output every year, and that's expected, not damage.

If your production is down and you aren't sure why, our guide on [solar degradation versus damage](/blog/solar-degradation-vs-damage/) helps tell the two apart. The difference matters because a claim needs a sudden cause you can point to, and a product warranty may be the better route for a defect.

Maintenance isn't covered either. Neither is damage from poor installation or from something that wasn't repaired for a long time. If a small problem gets worse, the carrier may argue it should have been fixed sooner.

## What happens after a claim

If hail takes out both your roof and your panels, the roof claim usually comes first, and the panels may be part of that or handled as a separate item. The solar contractor's job is to document the damage and give you a written, itemized quote, and the roofer or you typically handle the paperwork with the carrier.

## A short checklist before storm season

Do these now, while nothing is wrong.

1. Find your policy and read the sections on the dwelling, other structures, and covered perils.
2. Call your agent and confirm the carrier knows about the system.
3. Ask whether the limit reflects what it would cost to replace the roof and the array together.
4. Photograph the array and keep the install invoice, permit, and warranty documents in one place.
5. Ask what your deductible is for hail, since it's sometimes a percentage and not a flat amount.

Then if a storm does hit, you're calling about a known system and not explaining it for the first time.

## FAQ

### Does homeowners insurance cover solar panels on the roof?

Usually yes, as part of the dwelling, the same way the roof itself is covered. That's a general pattern and not a promise, so check your policy, confirm the dwelling limit reflects the system, and ask your carrier if anything specific applies to solar.

### Do I need to tell my insurance company I have solar panels?

Yes, it's a good idea. Coverage is set based on what the carrier knows about your property, and an unreported system may not be reflected in your limit. A short call to your agent when you add or buy a house with solar can prevent surprises during a claim.

### Who insures leased solar panels?

Generally the leasing company, since it owns the equipment, though your agreement spells out the details. You may still be responsible for the roof beneath the panels and for notifying the company about damage. Read the lease and ask both the company and your carrier.

### Are ground mount solar panels covered by homeowners insurance?

They may be, but often under other structures and not the dwelling, which can carry a separate limit and different terms. The same goes for panels on a barn or shop. Check your policy and ask your carrier how your array is classified.

### Does insurance cover solar panel degradation or wear?

Typically not. Homeowners policies generally cover sudden damage from perils like hail and wind, and they don't cover normal aging, gradual output loss, or defects. For a manufacturing problem, your product warranty is usually the better place to look.
