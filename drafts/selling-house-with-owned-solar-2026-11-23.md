# Selling a House With Solar Panels You Own: What Buyers Will Ask For

Selling a house with solar panels is easier than most owners fear, as long as the paperwork is in one folder before the first showing. Buyers like the idea of a system that's already paid for or financed. What they don't like is surprises, and a missing permit or an unknown roof age is the kind of surprise that slows a deal.

This page is for owned systems, meaning you paid cash or you're paying off a solar loan. If your panels are leased or sold under a power purchase agreement, that's a different process, and our guide to [leased solar panels and roof replacement](/blog/leased-solar-panels-roof-replacement/) covers who controls what. We'll leave it at that here.

We aren't real estate agents, lawyers, or lenders, so treat this as a practical checklist and not legal or real estate advice. Your agent, your title company, and your lender have the final say on how your sale works.

## The paperwork buyers and inspectors ask for

Start a folder, paper or digital, and gather these before you list. Most of it already exists somewhere in your email or your file cabinet.

- **The building or electrical permit** for the original install, and proof it passed final inspection.
- **The interconnection agreement** with your utility. In much of our area that's Xcel Energy, though some addresses are served by co-ops such as Poudre Valley REA or United Power.
- **Warranty documents** for the panels, the inverter, and the installer's workmanship. Don't summarize them from memory. Hand over the actual documents, because terms and transfer rules differ.
- **Production history.** Your monitoring app can usually export a year or more of output. Buyers and appraisers like a real number better than a salesperson's estimate.
- **System details:** panel count, inverter type, whether there's a battery, and the install date.
- **Roof information:** when the roof under the array was installed, and what material it is.

That last item is the one that surprises people, so it gets its own section below.

If the original installer is gone, you may have to rebuild the file from permit records and your own emails. Our post on [what happens when your solar installer goes out of business](/blog/solar-installer-out-of-business/) covers where to look.

## Does a buyer take over the warranties?

Sometimes, and it depends on the document. Some warranties follow the system and some follow the original owner, and some require the new owner to register or notify the manufacturer. We don't quote warranty terms because they vary by product and by year, and we're not going to guess at yours.

Read the transfer language in each warranty, or call the manufacturer or installer and ask. Write down the answer, with the date and who told you. A buyer's agent will ask.

## Solar loans, liens, and payoff

If you're still paying on a solar loan, the sale needs a plan for it. Depending on how the loan was set up, it may be unsecured, or it may be recorded against the property. Some loans can transfer to a buyer and many can't. That's a question for your lender and your title company, not something we can answer from here.

What we can say is practical. Ask your lender for a payoff statement early. Tell your agent and your title company that there's a solar loan so it doesn't turn up as a surprise at closing. And ask whether any lien or filing has been recorded, since that affects what has to be cleared before the sale closes.

None of this is unusual, and title companies deal with solar loans regularly. The trouble starts when nobody mentions it.

## The roof-age question

Here's the one that costs sellers real money and real time. A buyer's inspector will look at the roof, and if the panels sit on an older roof, the question comes up fast: what happens when this roof needs replacing?

The honest answer is that the panels have to come off and go back on. That's a detach and reset. Our explainer on [owning an old roof with solar panels](/blog/old-roof-with-solar-panels/) covers why a roof under an array ages differently and what to look for.

A buyer can't see the shingles under the panels, so they'll often assume the worst and ask for a credit. You have two sensible paths.

### Option one: reroof and reset before you list

You replace the roof, we detach and reset the array, and you list a house with a new roof and a system that's been recommissioned and tested. The roof question mostly goes away. You'll have a clean roof inspection and a specific install date to show.

The cost is that you spend money before the sale, and you're counting on the market to reward it. Whether that math works depends on your roof's condition, your timeline, and your agent's read of the local market. We can't tell you that part.

A typical 20-panel system runs around $3,500, and large or complex arrays can reach $6,000 to $8,000 and up. ### Option two: disclose and let the buyer price it

You list as is, tell the buyer the roof's age and that a detach and reset will be needed when it's replaced, and let them factor it in. Some buyers prefer this. They want to choose their own roofer and their own materials.

The risk is that an unknown is usually priced pessimistically. If you go this way, a written detach and reset quote in the buyer's hands helps a lot. ## Should you get an inspection before listing?

A solar contractor can inspect the array and give you a written condition note before you list. That's worth considering if your system has been through a hail season, if production has been slipping, or if you aren't sure the racking and wiring are in good shape.

Hail is the common reason. Weld County sits in one of the most hail-prone parts of the country, and a buyer's inspector who finds a cracked panel will ask what else is hiding. Our guide to [signs of hail damage on solar panels](/blog/signs-of-hail-damage-solar-panels/) shows what an owner can spot from the ground.

Knowing about damage before the buyer does gives you control over how it's handled. Finding out during their inspection period doesn't.

## What not to do

A few things we'd steer you away from.

Don't guess at figures in a listing. If you say the system produces a certain amount, have the production history to back it up.

Don't promise that incentives, tax benefits, or net metering terms carry over to the buyer. Those depend on your utility and your situation, and the rules change. If a buyer asks, point them to the utility and to a tax professional.

Don't skip the paperwork because the system is "obviously fine." A buyer's lender or insurer may ask for the documents regardless. Having them ready is cheaper than hunting for them under a closing deadline.

And don't wait on the roof decision until the inspection report comes back. By then you're negotiating from behind.

## A simple order of operations

If you're a few weeks from listing, this sequence works for most owners.

1. Gather the permit, interconnection agreement, warranties, and production history.
2. Get a payoff statement if there's a solar loan, and tell your title company.
3. Find out the age of the roof under the array.
4. Decide with your agent whether to reroof first or disclose.
5. If you reroof, book the detach and reset ahead of the roofer so the schedule holds.
6. Put the finished folder in the listing packet.

None of that is hard. It just takes a few weeks if you start late, and a few evenings if you start early.

## FAQ

### Do I have to remove my solar panels before selling my house?

No. Most buyers are happy to take a working system. Panels only need to come off if the roof is being replaced or the array needs repair, and that decision is separate from the sale itself. Talk to your agent about whether a reroof before listing makes sense for your house.

### Will a buyer take over my solar loan?

It depends on the loan terms and your lender. Some solar loans can transfer to a new owner and many are simply paid off at closing from the sale proceeds. Ask your lender for a payoff statement and ask your title company how it should be handled. We aren't lenders, so we can't answer that for your specific loan.

### What documents should I have ready for the buyer?

The permit and final inspection, the interconnection agreement with your utility, the warranty documents, a year or more of production history, the system details, and the age of the roof under the array. Having these in one folder before you list saves time when the buyer's inspector or lender asks.

### Should I replace my roof before I list a house with solar?

It depends on the roof's age and condition, your timeline, and your market. A new roof with a freshly reset array removes a big question for buyers, but it costs money up front. Disclosing the roof age and letting the buyer price the work is the other route. Your agent can tell you which one fits your area.

### What if my solar panels are leased instead of owned?

A lease or power purchase agreement is handled differently because the leasing company owns the equipment. The buyer usually has to qualify to take over the lease, and the company has its own process. Check your agreement and contact the company early, before you list.
