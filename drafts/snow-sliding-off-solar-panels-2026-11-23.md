# Snow Sliding Off Solar Panels: What to Expect on a Colorado Roof

Snow sliding off solar panels is one of the first surprises homeowners get after the array goes up. A roof that held snow quietly for years suddenly drops it in one loud sheet. If you're replacing the roof, this is the moment to decide whether that sheet needs somewhere to stop.

## Why snow comes off panels faster than shingles

Shingles are rough. They grip snow, and the snow sits there until the sun and the temperature get to it. Solar glass is smooth and slick, and the panels are often a little warmer than the roof around them once the sun hits.

When the glass warms, the bottom layer of snow loosens. With nothing holding it back, the whole layer can let go at once and slide down the panel face. It doesn't melt away gradually. It comes off as a slab.

That slab then keeps moving over whatever is below it: the lower roof section, the gutters, a porch, a walkway, a door, a parked car. You'll hear it before you see it, and on a bright morning after a heavy snow it can happen with no warning.

None of this means something is wrong with your panels or your roof. It's how smooth glass behaves under snow.

## What sliding snow can reach

Think about the path from your array to the ground, and what's on it. The usual places we see trouble:

- Gutters, which can get pulled loose, bent, or clogged by a slab of snow and ice.
- Walkways and entries below the array. A slab landing on a front step is a safety problem, not just a mess.
- Doors and garage openings under a roof edge.
- Lower roof sections, which take the load and can get overloaded with the added weight.
- Air conditioner units, utility meters, vents, and landscaping.

If you've lived with the array for a winter, you already know where your snow goes. If you haven't, look at the area under the roof edge before the next big storm and think about who or what stands there.

## Ice at the array edge

The lower edge of the array can collect ice. Meltwater from the warmer glass runs down and refreezes where it meets the cooler roof or the frame, and a ridge can build along the bottom.

Ice at the edge is a concern for two reasons. It can lift and stress the edge of the panel frame and the flashing below it. And when it finally lets go, it takes the snow above it along for the ride.

We don't recommend knocking it off. See the next section.

## Don't rake it, don't climb it

Short version: don't go onto the roof to clear panels, and don't use a roof rake on them.

Climbing onto a snowy roof is dangerous with or without solar. Add smooth glass, and the risk goes up. A roof rake or a shovel can scratch the glass and damage the frames or wiring, and pulling snow off by hand can bring a slab down on you.

Panels also shed on their own when the sun comes out, and the production you lose in the meantime is small compared to the risk. Winter production is lower in any case, and that's normal. Short days, a low sun, and snow cover all reduce output, and it comes back as the days lengthen. A low reading in January is not a sign the system is broken. If you're not sure, our post on [solar degradation versus damage](/blog/solar-degradation-vs-damage/) explains what's normal and what isn't.

If snow or ice is a hazard over an entry or walkway, the answer is to keep people out from under the roof edge until it's come down, not to go up and get it.

## What snow guards do

Snow guards are small barriers installed on the roof or at the lower edge of an array that hold snow in place so it melts or breaks up gradually instead of sliding off as one sheet. They come in a few styles, and the right one depends on the roof type, the array layout, and what's below.

They're not meant to keep snow on the roof forever. They're meant to slow and break up the slide so it doesn't drop as a single slab onto the gutter or the entry.

Two cautions. First, a snow guard adds a load point, and the way it's attached matters. It has to be fastened and flashed properly, or it becomes a leak path. Second, guards can change how snow collects at the array edge, so where they go is a design decision, not an afterthought.

## Front Range snow is different

Northern Colorado doesn't get the long, steady winter cover of some places. We get warm sun, then a storm, then warm sun again. That cycle is hard on arrays, because snow loads up quickly, then the sun comes out and it all comes off at once.

Spring is the one people forget. A wet, heavy snow in March or April can drop a lot of weight on a roof, and the panels are often warm enough by midday to shed it in a hurry. The result is a spring slide that's heavier than anything in January.

That same stretch of the year also overlaps with the start of hail season in Weld County, one of the most hail-prone parts of the country. A roof that's had a hard spring may need an inspection for more than snow. ## FAQ

### Why does snow slide off solar panels so fast?

The glass is smooth and often a little warmer than the roof around it. When the sun hits, the bottom layer of snow loosens and the whole slab can let go at once. Shingles are rough and hold snow longer, which is why a roof can behave differently after panels go on.

### Should I clear snow off my solar panels?

No. Don't climb onto a snowy roof, and don't use a roof rake or shovel on the glass, which can scratch the panels and damage frames or wiring. The panels shed on their own once the sun comes out. Lower winter production is normal, and it comes back as the days get longer.

### What are snow guards and do I need them?

Snow guards are barriers at the lower edge of the array or on the roof that hold snow back so it breaks up and melts gradually instead of dropping as one sheet. The need depends on what sits below the array. They're best decided at reroof time, when the roof is open, and we quote them in writing.

### Can ice at the edge of the array damage my panels?

It can stress the frame and the flashing at the lower edge, and when it lets go it can take a lot of snow with it. Don't knock it off yourself. Keep people clear of the area below, and if you're concerned, have the array looked at when the roof is next open.

### Do solar panels still produce in winter in Northern Colorado?

Yes, but less. Short days, a low sun, and snow cover all reduce output, so lower numbers in winter are normal and not a sign of a fault. Production recovers as the days lengthen and the snow clears. If numbers stay low in clear weather, that's the time to have the system checked.
