# Solar Panel Degradation vs Damage: How to Tell the Difference

Your solar app shows a number you don't like. Production looks lower than it did last year, and you're wondering if that's just age or whether something's wrong. It's a fair question, and the answer usually comes down to the shape of the drop. Slow and steady points one way. Sudden points the other.

This page walks through solar panel degradation vs damage: what's normal, what the weather and the neighborhood do to your numbers, and how to compare your own data in a way that tells you something. We aren't going to quote percentages here, because yours depend on your panels and your paperwork.

## What normal degradation looks like

Every panel loses a little output as it ages. That's expected, and it's gradual. The loss is spread across years, not dropped on you in one month.

Manufacturers typically warrant a degradation curve, a schedule of how much output the panels should still be producing at different ages. The specifics differ from one brand and model to the next. Check your own warranty document or datasheet for yours. We won't guess at a number, because a made-up one would be worse than none.

What you can say in general is this: normal degradation is small from year to year, and it affects the whole array at roughly the same rate. You'd never see one panel fall off a cliff while the others stay put. And you wouldn't see it show up on a specific day.

If your production slipped slightly compared with last year, and it's been that way gently since the system went in, that's likely this slow curve doing its thing.

## What the weather does to your numbers

Before you blame the panels, remember that production swings for ordinary reasons. Sunshine is not a constant.

- **Seasons.** Production is lower in winter and higher in summer, so compare like with like.
- **Cloud cover.** A cloudy month is a low month. Some years simply have more clouds than others.
- **Smoke and haze.** Wildfire smoke can cut production in a bad summer.
- **Snow.** A panel under snow produces little until it clears. Our post on [snow sliding off solar panels](/blog/snow-sliding-off-solar-panels/) covers what happens when it comes down.
- **Heat.** Panels are less efficient when they're very hot, which is why a bright summer day doesn't always beat a cold, clear spring one.

A year-to-year change of a modest size can come from any of these. It's the reason a single week or even a single month isn't proof of anything.

## Dirt, dust, and what the fields send your way

Here in Weld County, dirt is a bigger factor than many people expect. We have dry soil, farm fields being worked, dirt roads, and feedlots, and all of that puts dust and grime in the air. It settles on glass.

A layer of dust blocks a little light on every panel. A bad coat of dirt can cut production by a noticeable amount, and it's the kind of drop that tends to be gradual and fairly even across the array. Rain and snow wash some of it off, and you can often see production rebound after a good storm.

Bird droppings and leaves are different: they sit on one spot and can shade a few cells. That can reduce a panel's output more than the size of the spot suggests. If your app shows individual panel data, a single panel that runs below its neighbors can point to something like this. Check your manual for cleaning guidance first, and don't climb on the roof to do it yourself.

Dirt is cheap to rule out, so it's worth thinking about before you worry about damage.

## What sudden drops look like

Damage tends to look different from slow decline. It's abrupt.

A drop that starts on a specific date is the red flag. If your production was normal up to a storm and dropped after it, hail is a suspect. The same goes for a roof job. If you saw a step down after a reroof, something may have been disturbed.

Common patterns:

- **After hail.** Panels with hidden microcracks can lose output right away, or slowly in the weeks after. Our post on [microcracks from hail](/blog/solar-panel-micro-cracks-hail/) explains why you may not see anything on the glass. Our guide to [signs of hail damage on solar panels](/blog/signs-of-hail-damage-solar-panels/) lists what to check.
- **After a reroof.** A drop can come from a loose connector, a shaded panel, a tripped breaker, or a reset that wasn't finished properly. Our post on [solar panels producing less after reinstall](/blog/solar-panels-producing-less-after-reinstall/) covers the common causes.
- **One string or one panel.** If a section of the array drops and the rest stays normal, that points to a wiring or panel issue and not general aging.
- **A hot spot.** A cell that's damaged can run hotter than the rest and lose output. Read about [solar panel hot spots](/blog/solar-panel-hot-spots/) if you suspect this.

Slow decline is the whole array getting a little worse together. A sudden drop is a specific event with a date on it.

## How to compare production the right way

The most useful test you can run is simple, and you can do it from your couch. Compare the same month year over year.

Here's how:

1. Open your monitoring app and find the monthly production view.
2. Pick a month, say last October, and look at its total.
3. Look at the same month this year.
4. Do the same for the month before and after, so you can see the pattern.
5. Note anything that changed: a storm, a roof job, a tree that grew, a new neighbor building.

Same month to same month takes the seasons out of the picture. If the numbers are close, you're likely seeing ordinary variation. If this year is noticeably lower and you can't explain it with weather, that's worth a closer look.

Two cautions. First, a cloudy month can look like a problem. If you can, compare a few months, and compare days with similar weather. Second, if your app went offline for part of the month, the total will be low for the wrong reason. Our post on [solar monitoring that keeps going offline](/blog/solar-monitoring-keeps-going-offline/) covers that.

If you have panel-level data, look at the individual panels too. A single panel well below its neighbors is more informative than a total.

## When to get it looked at

Here's a rough way to decide:

- **Gentle, even decline over years:** probably normal. Check your warranty curve and keep an eye on it.
- **Lower after a dusty, dry stretch:** probably dirt. Ask about cleaning.
- **A sudden step down after a storm or roof job:** get it looked at.
- **One panel or one string behaving differently:** get it looked at.
- **Error codes on the inverter:** get it looked at.

## FAQ

### How much do solar panels degrade each year?

It depends on the panel. Manufacturers typically warrant a degradation curve that shows how much output they expect over time, and it differs by brand and model. Check your warranty document or datasheet for yours. What's true in general is that normal degradation is slow and affects the whole array evenly.

### How can I tell if my solar drop is damage and not age?

Look at the shape of it. Gradual, even decline over years is typical of aging. A sudden drop starting on a specific date, especially after hail or a roof job, or one panel or string behaving differently from the rest, points to damage or a wiring problem.

### Does dirt really affect solar production in Northern Colorado?

Yes. Dust from fields, dirt roads, and feedlots settles on the glass and blocks some light. It usually causes a gradual, even drop, and production often recovers after rain or snow washes it off. Bird droppings can shade a spot on one panel, which can hit output harder than you'd expect.

### How do I compare solar production year over year?

Open your monitoring app, find the monthly totals, and compare the same month this year against last year. That removes the seasons from the picture. Look at neighboring months too, and keep weather in mind, since a cloudy or smoky month will read low even on a healthy system.

### Should I be worried if production dropped after hail?

It's worth checking. Hail can cause microcracks under glass that looks fine, and those can reduce output right away or over the following weeks. Save your production data from before and after the storm and have the array inspected rather than waiting to see if it recovers.
