# Solar Installer Out of Business? What Still Works and What to Do Next

You call the company that put solar on your roof, and the number is disconnected. Or the website is gone. Or you find out from a neighbor. If your solar installer is out of business, it's natural to assume the whole system is now on its own, and the warranty went down with the company.

Mostly, that's not how it works. Your panels are still producing. The equipment still has a maker behind it. What you've lost is the person who knew your system, and that gap matters most when a roof replacement or a hail storm shows up. Here's how to sort out what you still have.

## What still works when the installer is gone

Start with the good news. Your system doesn't need the installer to keep making power. The panels, inverter, and racking are physical equipment, and they keep doing their job.

Your warranties are the part that needs a closer look. In general, there are two layers, and they don't always end up in the same place:

- **Equipment warranties.** The company that made your panels and the company that made your inverter or microinverters usually stand behind their own products. Those warranties typically belong to the equipment, not to the installer, so they're often still in force when an installer closes.
- **Workmanship warranty.** This is the installer's own promise about the install itself, including roof penetrations and wiring. When the installer is gone, this layer is usually the one that's gone with it.

We say "usually" on purpose. Terms differ from one product and one contract to the next, and we can't tell you what yours says. Read your warranty documents, and if you can't find them, contact the equipment manufacturer directly with your serial numbers. Ask them whether the warranty is still active and whether it transfers or has conditions attached.

## Finding your paperwork

Most people with an orphaned system think they have nothing. You probably have more than you think. Check these places in roughly this order:

1. **Your original contract and closing folder.** Look for the purchase agreement, equipment list, and any warranty booklets. If the system was financed, the lender may also hold a copy.
2. **The monitoring app or portal.** Whatever you use to watch production usually shows the inverter brand and model, and sometimes the panel count and install date. Take screenshots now. Our post on [solar monitoring that keeps going offline](/blog/solar-monitoring-keeps-going-offline/) covers what to do when that app stops reporting.
3. **City or county permit records.** The building permit for your install is a public record in most jurisdictions. It often lists the panel model, the inverter, the racking, and the electrical details. Your local building department can usually pull it by address.
4. **Your utility's interconnection file.** When your system was approved to connect, your utility kept an application with the equipment list and system size. Whether that's Xcel Energy or a co-op in your part of Weld or Larimer County, your utility can usually tell you what's on file.
5. **The equipment itself.** Panels have a label on the back, and the inverter has a nameplate. If you can't safely see them, a photo from the ground with a zoom lens or a look at the inverter cabinet can fill in the gaps.

Put everything into one folder, digital or paper. You'll want it for any warranty claim, any insurance conversation, and any future roof work.

## Who can service an orphaned system

Here's the practical question. If something goes wrong, who do you call?

Equipment manufacturers sometimes maintain lists of certified service providers, and they'll often point you to one. Other solar contractors can also work on a system they didn't install. That's common in the trade. A good one will start by looking at what's there, because they can't assume the previous company followed the manual.

A few things to ask any contractor before they touch your array:

- Have they worked on this brand of racking and inverter before?
- Will they put the findings and the scope of work in writing?
- Will they explain what they're taking responsibility for, and what they aren't?

Our post on [how to choose a solar detach and reset contractor](/blog/choosing-solar-detach-reset-contractor/) goes through that vetting in more detail. The short version is to ask for a written scope and to be careful about anyone who promises a fix before they've seen the roof.

## Why a reroof is the moment this really matters

An orphaned system can run for years without much attention. The trouble starts when the roof underneath it needs to be replaced, because the panels have to come off and go back on. That's when you find out whether anyone is available to do the electrical side, and whether anyone is willing to put their name on the reinstall.

If your roof is aging, start planning before it leaks. A written estimate early gives you time to choose without a deadline.

## Leased systems are a different story

Everything above assumes you own your system. If you lease it or have a power purchase agreement, the lease company, not the installer, is usually the owner of the panels. That changes the situation a lot. You typically can't decide on your own to remove or reset the array, and the lease company usually has its own process and its own service network, whatever happened to the original installer.

Read your lease agreement and call the company named on it. Our post on [leased solar panels and roof replacement](/blog/leased-solar-panels-roof-replacement/) explains the questions to ask before anyone schedules roofers.

## Keep the record from here on

Whatever you decide, the best thing you can do now is document the system as it stands. Take clear photos of the array from the ground and from any safe vantage point. Photograph the inverter, the meter, and the disconnect. Screenshot your monitoring data. Save your permit and interconnection records in the same place.

That file does three jobs. It speeds up any warranty conversation with a manufacturer. It gives a contractor the starting information they need for an accurate quote. And if the system ever gets damaged in a hail storm, it shows what was there beforehand.

An orphaned system is rarely a lost cause. It's a system that needs someone new in the picture, and a clear set of records makes that handoff a lot easier.

## FAQ

### Is my solar warranty void if the installer closed?

Not automatically. Panel and inverter warranties usually come from the equipment manufacturers, so they often continue after an installer closes. The installer's own workmanship warranty is the part that most often ends with the company. Check your warranty documents and contact the manufacturer with your serial numbers to confirm what's still active.

### Where can I find the paperwork for a solar system I didn't install?

Look through your original contract, your closing folder, and your monitoring app first. Then ask your city or county building department for the permit on your address, and ask your utility for the interconnection file. Between those sources you can usually reconstruct the panel model, inverter model, system size, and install date.

### Who can service my solar panels now?

Other solar contractors can generally work on systems they didn't install, and equipment manufacturers sometimes keep lists of approved service providers. Ask any contractor whether they've worked with your brand of inverter and racking, and get their findings and scope in writing before work starts.

### Can I still do a detach and reset if the installer is gone?

Yes. A detach and reset doesn't require the original installer. The panels come off, the roof is replaced, and the array goes back on new flashed mounts with a full recommissioning and production test. What matters is that the contractor inspects the racking and electrical side before quoting.

### What if my system is leased and the company that installed it closed?

With a lease or power purchase agreement, the lease company is usually the owner of the equipment, not the installer. Read your agreement and contact the company named on it. They typically control any removal or reset, and they'll have their own process for it.
