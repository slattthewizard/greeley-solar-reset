# Solar Panel Hail Rating: What the Test Actually Covers

If you live in Weld County, you've probably heard that solar panels are "hail rated." That's true, as far as it goes. The trouble is how far it goes, and whether the number on the spec sheet matches the storms we get along the Front Range.

This page explains the solar panel hail rating in plain terms: what the lab test is, what it isn't, and why a panel can pass the test and still end up with damage after a bad afternoon in Greeley. We aren't a manufacturer or an insurance adviser, so we'll stick to how the test works and what we see on roofs.

## What the solar panel hail rating really means

Panels sold in the US are typically certified to IEC 61215 and UL 61730. Those standards bundle together a long list of durability tests, and one of them is a hail impact test.

Here's how it works. A lab fires a solid ice ball, 25 mm across (about 1 inch), at the panel at roughly 23 meters per second. That's around 51 mph. The ball hits a set of specific spots on the glass, including the corners and edges. After the impacts, the panel gets inspected and its electrical output is measured. If it doesn't show visible damage and hasn't lost more than the allowed amount of power, it passes.

That's it. One ball size, one speed, a set of spots, a pass or fail.

So when someone says a panel is "hail rated," they usually mean it passed that test. It's a floor, a baseline that panels have to clear to be certified. It isn't a promise that the panel survives any hailstorm.

## Why Northern Colorado hail can beat the test

Weld County sits in one of the most hail-prone parts of the country. Our storms don't read the spec sheet.

A 1 inch stone is about the size of a quarter. Hail bigger than that happens here. Some storms drop stones the size of golf balls, and some are larger. A bigger stone carries far more energy than a small one, because energy climbs quickly with both size and weight. Add wind pushing the stones sideways and the hit is harder still.

There's another gap between the lab and your roof. In the lab, the ice ball is fired straight at a panel lying flat and supported evenly. On a real roof, the panel is bolted to rails at certain points, tilted at the pitch of your roof, and aimed at by stones coming in at an angle with the wind behind them. Hail in a thunderstorm also doesn't arrive one ball at a time. A panel can take dozens of hits in a few minutes.

None of that means panels are fragile. Most arrays in this area come through ordinary hail just fine. But if you've seen a storm that left dimples in car hoods and shredded garden leaves, the test ball size is no longer the relevant number.

## Tempered glass: strong, but it fails differently

The glass on the front of a panel is tempered. It's heat treated so it's much tougher than ordinary window glass, and that's a big part of why panels pass the hail test.

Tempered glass has one trait worth knowing. When it does fail, it doesn't chip or dent the way a car panel does. It can go from looking perfect to shattering across the whole sheet. Sometimes you see a spiderweb pattern radiating from one point. Sometimes a single hit fractures the entire face of the glass.

That's also why a cracked panel is easy to spot and an unbroken one gives you no guarantee. ## How a panel can pass the test and still get damaged

This is the part that surprises most homeowners. A panel can survive a hailstorm with perfect glass and still be hurt.

The glass is only the cover. Underneath it sits a layer of silicon cells, and they're thin and brittle. A hard impact can flex the glass just enough to fracture a cell without cracking the glass at all. These hairline fractures are called microcracks. You usually can't see them from the ground, or even from the roof in daylight.

Microcracks matter because of what they do over time. A cracked cell can lose electrical contact in part of its area. That can drop a panel's output, and in some cases it makes a spot run hotter than the cells around it. Production loss from microcracks tends to show up slowly rather than all at once, which makes it easy to miss. We walk through the whole mechanism in [how microcracks from hail affect solar panels](/blog/solar-panel-micro-cracks-hail/).

The lab test accounts for this to a degree, since it measures output after the impacts. But a pass in the lab means a sample panel held up against 1 inch stones. It says nothing about what a bigger stone, or the sixtieth stone, did to the cells on your roof.

## What this means after a storm

Here's the practical takeaway. A hail rating tells you the panel met a minimum standard. It doesn't tell you your array is fine after a specific storm.

So after hail, don't wait for glass to break before you pay attention. A few things are worth doing:

- Look at your production in the monitoring app for the days after the storm and compare it to a normal stretch.
- Check the panels from the ground with binoculars, if it's safe and the roof is dry. Never climb up there.
- Look for chips, cracks, shattered corners, or bent frames.
- Check for error messages on the inverter.

## What we can say about specific brands

Nothing in this article is a claim that one brand's panel is tougher than another. Manufacturers publish their own test data, and some test beyond the standard. If you want to know what your panel was tested to, the datasheet and your warranty document are the places to check. Read what they say about hail and what they exclude. Some warranties treat hail as an act of nature and handle it differently from a manufacturing defect, so it's worth reading rather than assuming.

## Roof shingles have a rating too, and it's a different one

It's easy to mix up the two. Impact-resistant shingles are rated under UL 2218, and Class 4 is the top rating. That test uses steel balls dropped from a set height, a different method from the ice ball test panels face.

So a Class 4 roof and a hail-tested panel are rated by different standards, and the numbers don't compare directly. If you're picking shingles for a reroof under an array, we cover that in our post on [Class 4 shingles with solar panels](/blog/class-4-shingles-with-solar-panels/).

## FAQ

### What size hail do solar panels have to survive?

The standard hail test fires a 25 mm ice ball, about 1 inch, at roughly 23 meters per second, which is around 51 mph. Panels certified to IEC 61215 and UL 61730 have to pass that test. It's a minimum bar rather than a limit, and larger hail can and does damage panels that passed it.

### Are solar panels hail proof?

No. Tempered glass is tough, and most panels handle ordinary hail well, but no panel is proof against every storm. Stones bigger than the test ball, wind-driven hail, and repeated hits can all cause damage. Some of that damage is a visible crack, and some is hidden microcracking in the cells under intact glass.

### Can a solar panel be damaged if the glass isn't cracked?

Yes. The cells under the glass are thin and brittle, and an impact can fracture them without breaking the glass on top. These microcracks often can't be seen by eye. They can show up later as lower output on a panel or as a hot spot, which is why checking your production after a storm matters.

### Does a higher hail rating mean my panels are safe in Greeley?

Not necessarily. Weld County gets some of the most severe hail in the country, and storms here can drop stones larger than the 1 inch test ball. A panel that passed the standard test is a reasonable starting point, but your datasheet, your warranty, and an inspection after a storm tell you more than a rating does.

### How do I find out what my panels were tested to?

Check the datasheet for your panel model, which usually lists the certifications it carries, and read your warranty document for how it treats hail. If you can't find them, the manufacturer or your original installer can send copies. We don't make claims about any brand, so we'd point you to the paperwork rather than guess.
