# Do Solar Panels Need to Be Removed for Gutter Replacement or Other Roof-Edge Work?

Most of the time, no. If you're asking whether solar panels need to be removed for gutter replacement, the usual answer is that a gutter crew can work around the array just fine. But there are a handful of exceptions, and a few other small jobs near the panels, like skylights, chimney flashing, and vents, that behave differently. This page sorts out which is which.

If you're dealing with a full roof replacement, that's a different question, and our guide to [roof repair without removing solar panels](/blog/roof-repair-without-removing-solar-panels/) covers where the line sits between a repair and a job that needs a detach.

## Gutters: usually no removal

Gutters hang off the edge of the roof at the eave, and the lowest row of panels normally sits back from that edge. A gutter installer works from a ladder or from the ground, and doesn't need to touch the array. For a straightforward replacement, the panels stay where they are.

There are a few situations where that changes.

**The bottom row overhangs or sits very close to the eave.** Some arrays are placed close to the edge. If the panels or the rail ends extend over the eave, the crew may not have room to hang the new gutter and fasteners without hitting them.

**The eave itself needs work.** If the fascia, drip edge, or the roof edge is rotten or damaged, fixing it may mean getting at the underside of the lowest panels. That's a repair on the roof, and it can mean a partial detach.

**Gutter guards or a new roof edge profile.** Some guards and edge products tuck under the first course of shingles, which can be right where the array's mounts are.

**Snow and ice damage.** If a snow slide took out the old gutter, the cause might be the array itself. Our post on [snow sliding off solar panels](/blog/snow-sliding-off-solar-panels/) explains why that happens and what to do about it.

The practical step is simple. Before the gutter crew shows up, tell them there's an array and where its lowest edge sits. Walk the eave with them if you can.

## Skylight replacement

Skylights are a different story. A skylight is a hole in the roof with flashing built into the shingles, and replacing one means working in the surrounding roof surface. If the array sits right next to the skylight, the crew needs room to lift shingles, rework the flashing, and re-seal.

If there's a clear gap between the skylight and the nearest panel, the skylight crew can often work without touching the array. If the panels are tight to the curb, one or two may need to come off temporarily.

Be careful about who decides this. A skylight installer isn't a solar installer, and walking across panels or levering shingles near mounts can damage the attachment points or the wiring. The mounts are flashed, and disturbing the shingle around them can start a leak. If you're seeing water already, read our post on [roof leaks under solar panels](/blog/roof-leak-under-solar-panels/).

## Chimney and vent flashing

Chimney flashing runs up and around the brick or the chase, and it's another spot where water gets in when it fails. If the chimney sits near the array, the repair may need access to the roof surface next to the panels.

Plumbing vents, attic vents, and exhaust vents are smaller jobs, but they're penetrations, and the same logic applies. A vent a few feet from the panels is usually fine to repair with the array in place. A vent tucked under the lowest row isn't.

In general, if the work is on the roof surface right next to the array and the roofer can't reach it without stepping on or lifting a panel, it's a candidate for a partial detach.

## Satellite dishes and other attachments

Old satellite dishes are a common one. They're often bolted to the roof or the fascia, and removing them means pulling the mount and sealing the holes. If the dish sits within a few feet of the panels, there's usually no need to move the array. Just make sure whoever pulls it knows where the wiring runs.

Antennas, lightning rods, and solar water heater panels are the same kind of thing. Ask the installer or remover to look at the array first.

## When a partial detach is enough

Here's what surprises people. Not every job near the panels means taking the whole array off. If only a few panels are in the way, they can usually be detached individually, because each panel is clamped to the rails on its own. That's often called a partial detach.

A partial detach means disconnecting and removing only the panels covering the work area, setting them aside safely, and putting them back when the other trade is done. The rest of the system stays up. On some systems, taking out one panel affects the string or the whole circuit, so the electrical side still needs care, and the system generally needs to be shut down during the work.

## Ask the other trade to call before stepping near the array

This is the habit that saves the most grief. Before any contractor gets on your roof, ask them to call you or us first if they find the work reaches the panels.

Here's what to tell them.

- There's a solar array, and here's where it sits.
- Don't walk on the panels, and don't pry near the mounts.
- Don't cut or move any wiring, conduit, or conduit supports.
- Stop and call if the repair requires touching or moving a panel.

A roofer or handyman who isn't used to solar can damage a panel or break a seal in a couple of seconds. If that happens, our guide on [who pays when a roofer damages solar panels](/blog/roofer-damaged-solar-panels-who-pays/) explains how it usually gets sorted out.

It also helps to get the plan in writing. If the other trade says they'll work around the array, have that noted in their quote, so everyone knows who's responsible if something goes wrong.

## A quick way to decide

Ask yourself two questions. First, can the other trade reach the work without touching or standing on a panel? If yes, the array can usually stay put. Second, is the work close enough to the mounts that disturbing the roof could cause a leak? If yes, call a solar contractor before the job starts.

## FAQ

### Do solar panels need to be removed for gutter replacement?

Usually not. Gutters hang at the eave, and the lowest row of panels normally sits back from that edge, so the crew can work around the array. Exceptions are when the bottom row overhangs the eave, or when the fascia or roof edge needs repair underneath the panels.

### Can a skylight be replaced with solar panels nearby?

Often yes, if there's a clear gap between the skylight and the nearest panel. If the array is tight to the skylight curb, one or two panels may need to come off temporarily so the crew can rework the flashing. The decision should be made by someone who understands the array.

### What is a partial solar detach?

It's removing only the panels that cover the work area and leaving the rest of the system in place. Each panel is clamped to the rails on its own, so a few can usually come off without disturbing the whole array. The system generally needs to be shut down during the work and tested afterward.

### Should I tell other contractors about my solar panels?

Yes, before they get on the roof. Tell them where the array is, ask them not to walk on the panels or touch the wiring, and ask them to call before stepping near it if the work reaches the panels. That one conversation prevents most of the damage we hear about.

### Can a chimney flashing repair be done with the panels still on?

Sometimes. If the chimney is a few feet from the array, the roofer can usually work with the panels in place. If the flashing runs under or right beside a panel, a partial detach may be needed. A solar contractor can look at the layout and tell you before the job starts.
