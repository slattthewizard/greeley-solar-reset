# Wind Damage to Solar Panels on the Front Range

Wind damage to solar panels doesn't get the attention hail does, but along the Front Range it's a real one. We get downslope wind events off the foothills that can run for hours, and an array sits on your roof like a sail with a few bolts holding it down.

Most arrays come through fine. This page is for when you aren't sure, when a storm just went through, or when you're mid-reroof and want to know what to look at.

## How wind hurts an array

Panels are tested and mounted to resist wind, but resisting isn't the same as being immune, and the weak points are usually small ones.

Wind can do several things to an array:

- Lift panels at the edges, working them against the clamps that hold them down.
- Loosen racking hardware, especially where bolts and clamps have corroded or been tightened unevenly.
- Fail a clamp outright, which lets a panel shift or lift.
- Stress the attachment points where the racking meets the roof, which can open a path for water.
- Throw debris at the glass: branches, roofing pieces, patio furniture, trash cans.

The edges of the array take the most exposure. Panels along the roof edge, the ridge, and the corners see the strongest uplift, because wind speeds up and swirls around those zones. A problem at one corner doesn't mean the whole array is loose, but it's where we look first.

## Front Range wind is its own thing

Northern Colorado's worst wind often arrives as a downslope event. Air comes down off the mountains and accelerates across the plains, and it can be gusty and sustained over many hours. It doesn't need a thunderstorm to do damage.

Wind can also come with the storms that bring hail. If your area had hail, read our post on the [signs of hail damage on solar panels](/blog/signs-of-hail-damage-solar-panels/) alongside this one, because the two can overlap and a damaged panel might have been hit by both.

Our guidance isn't to guess at wind speeds. It's to check the array after any big event, especially one with long, strong gusts.

## What you can check from the ground

Stay off the roof. The array is live whenever there's light, and a roof after a storm may be wet, damaged, or covered in debris. Everything below can be done from the yard or the driveway.

Look for:

- A panel that sits at a different angle than its neighbors, or one with a visible gap at the edge.
- Panels that appear lifted, shifted, or no longer lined up in the row.
- Sections of racking or rail that look bent or out of place.
- A loose cable hanging below the array edge.
- Debris stuck against or under the panels.
- Cracked glass or visible impact marks.
- Shingles missing or lifted around the array, or flashing that's moved.
- Pieces of the system on the ground, like clamp covers, end caps, or fragments of a panel.

Take photos from several angles and a few zoomed in, so the problems are documented before anyone touches anything. A phone with a decent zoom does well for this, and binoculars help more than climbing.

## If something looks wrong

Treat a damaged array as live. A cracked panel can still produce power in daylight, and a loose cable can be energized.

Here's the safe sequence:

1. Stay off the roof and away from anything that's fallen. Don't touch loose panels or cables.
2. Use the system's rapid shutdown or the AC disconnect, if you know where they are and it's safe to reach them. Our guide on [how to shut off solar after a storm](/blog/how-to-shut-off-solar-after-storm/) walks through this step by step.
3. Photograph the damage from the ground.
4. Call us. For active storm damage, our [emergency solar service in Greeley](/emergency-solar-service-greeley/) covers the response.

After a wind event, documentation is what moves a claim. We aren't adjusters and we don't advise on claims, but we can tell you what's useful.

Gather:

- Dated photos of the array, the roof, and any debris, taken from the ground.
- Photos of anything that landed on the roof or the panels.
- The date and a description of the weather event.
- Your monitoring data, if you have it, showing a drop in production or a system fault around that date.
- A written quote from a solar contractor that itemizes what needs repair or replacement.

## Why the reroof is the time to deal with loose hardware

Racking hardware lives outdoors for decades. Bolts, clamps, and rails go through freeze and thaw, rain, sun, and wind. Some corrode, some work loose, and a few never got tightened to spec in the first place.

You can't judge any of that from the ground. When the array comes off for a reroof, every clamp, rail, and bolt is in our hands. That's when we can see which parts are tight, which are worn, and which are rusting.

As part of every reset, we inspect the racking and replace corroded hardware. We also mount the array on new flashed mounts at the attachment points instead of reusing the old penetrations, so the connection between the roof and the racking is fresh.

If your array went through a few hard wind seasons and nobody has looked at the hardware since it was installed, a reroof is the cheapest time to change it out. ## What not to do

A few don'ts:

- Don't climb onto the roof to inspect, tighten, or reposition anything.
- Don't try to push a lifted panel back down.
- Don't leave a damaged array running if it looks unsafe. Shut it off and call.
- Don't let someone tell you a loose panel is "fine" without looking at the hardware.
- Don't wait out the next windstorm on a system you know is loose.

A small problem with a clamp or a rail is cheap to fix when it's found. The same problem after a panel lifts off in the next big blow is a different job.

## FAQ

### Can wind blow solar panels off a roof?

It can in severe cases, usually when a clamp, rail, or attachment has failed or corroded. Most arrays hold up fine. Edge and corner panels take the most uplift, so those are the ones to watch. If you see a panel sitting at a different angle or a gap at the edge, shut the system down and call.

### How do I check for wind damage without going on the roof?

Look from the ground with binoculars or a phone zoom. Check for panels out of line, gaps, bent rails, loose cables, debris against the array, cracked glass, and missing shingles or flashing nearby. Take photos from several angles. Never climb up to look, because the array is live in daylight and the roof may be damaged.

### What should I do if a panel looks loose or lifted?

Stay off the roof, keep people clear of the area below, and treat everything as live. Shut the system off at the disconnect if you can reach it safely, photograph the damage from the ground, and call for storm-damage service. Don't try to push the panel back down or tighten anything yourself.

### Does insurance cover wind damage to solar panels?

That depends on your policy, so ask your carrier. What helps any claim is documentation: dated photos from the ground, a description of the weather event, monitoring data showing a production drop, and an itemized written quote for the repair. We aren't adjusters and can't tell you what your policy covers.

### Should I replace racking hardware when the roof is replaced?

If it's corroded or loose, yes. A reroof is the best chance to inspect every clamp, rail, and bolt up close, since the whole array is off the roof. We inspect the racking at every reset, replace corroded hardware, and set the array back on new flashed mounts instead of reusing the old penetrations.
