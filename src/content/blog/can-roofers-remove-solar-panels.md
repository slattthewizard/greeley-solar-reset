Your roofer just walked your hail-hammered roof, handed you a bid, and then pointed at your solar array and said "those have to come off first, and that's not us." Now you're stuck mid-claim wondering who actually does that part.

So can roofers remove solar panels? In most cases, no, and the reasons aren't just company policy. In Colorado, disconnecting and reconnecting a grid-tied solar system is electrical work, and roofing crews generally aren't licensed, insured, or trained to touch it. The few who say yes are usually subcontracting it out anyway, or worse, sending shingle guys up to unbolt live equipment.

Here's what's really going on, what it should cost, and how to line up the solar work so your reroof doesn't stall for two weeks with panels stacked in your garage.

## Why Your Roofer Keeps Saying "That's Not Us"

Roofers in Weld and Larimer counties see solar arrays on a big share of the roofs they bid now. Most of them still won't touch the panels, and it comes down to four things.

**Licensing.** A grid-tied solar system is wired into your main electrical panel. Shutting it down, disconnecting it, and re-energizing it later is electrical work under Colorado law, and that work runs through licensed electricians registered with the state. You can look up how Colorado regulates this through the [Colorado Division of Professions and Occupations electrical board](https://dpo.colorado.gov/Electrical). A roofing license doesn't cover any of it.

**Insurance.** Roofers carry general liability for roofing. If a crew member drops a panel, cross-wires a string, or backfeeds something they shouldn't, their policy likely won't respond because they were performing work outside their trade. That leaves you holding the bag.

**Warranties.** Panel and inverter manufacturers can deny warranty claims if unqualified people handled the equipment. Your installer's workmanship warranty often has the same language. One roofing crew's afternoon of "helping" can quietly void protection on a $25,000 system.

**Liability they can see coming.** Experienced roofers have watched this movie. They know a cracked cell or a torqued connector doesn't always show up until months later, and they don't want that phone call.

So when your roofer says "get your solar guy," they're not dodging work. They're telling you the truth about where their lane ends. ## Can Roofers Remove Solar Panels Legally in Colorado?

Legally, a roofing company can't perform the electrical side of solar removal unless they have licensed electricians on staff, and almost none do. That's the short answer. The longer answer is where homeowners get burned, because "removal" is really three jobs bundled together.

First is the electrical disconnect. Someone has to shut down the inverter, open the AC and DC disconnects, verify the system is de-energized, and safely cap or protect the wiring that stays behind. Grid-tied systems feed power back toward the utility, so this isn't like unplugging a lamp. The Department of Energy has a good plain-language overview of how [grid-connected home solar systems](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/grid-connected-renewable-energy-systems) work if you want the background.

Second is the physical removal. Panels come off the rails, rails come off the mounts, and everything gets labeled and stored so it goes back exactly where it was. Modern panels are tougher than they look but they don't tolerate being carried flat, stacked wrong, or leaned against a fence for two weeks.

Third is the reinstall and recommissioning. After your new roof is on, the mounts go back with proper flashing, the array gets rebuilt, and the system gets re-energized and tested. Skip the testing step and you may not find out for months that one string never came back online. We wrote about that exact problem in [why solar systems end up offline after roof replacement](/blog/solar-system-offline-after-roof-replacement/).

A roofing crew can sometimes legally do part of job two. They can't do jobs one and three. And splitting one continuous process across two companies that don't talk to each other is how panels get damaged and warranties get contested.

Take Mike in Evans. His roofer said they'd "handle everything" after last summer's hail. The roofer subbed the panel work to a general labor crew, who stacked seventeen panels face-down on his driveway. Three came back with cracked glass and two more had micro-crack damage that showed up in production numbers later. The labor crew's insurance denied the claim, the roofer pointed at the sub, and Mike ate about $3,800 replacing panels his insurer had already paid to protect.

## What a Proper Detach and Reset Looks Like

A legitimate detach and reset crew treats your system like it has to come back at 100 percent, because it does. Here's the sequence you should expect. We break the full process down in [what is solar detach and reset](/blog/what-is-solar-detach-and-reset/), but the short version looks like this.

Before anything comes loose, the crew documents baseline production, photographs the array and racking layout, and maps every string. Then the system gets shut down in the correct order, verified dead, and disconnected by someone qualified to do it.

Panels come off one at a time, get inspected, labeled, and stored on edge in a protected spot, usually your garage. Rails and mounts come off next, and old mount holes get noted so the roofer can seal them properly.

Then the roofers do their thing. A good solar crew coordinates directly with the roofing foreman on flashing and mount placement so the new roof's warranty and the array's attachment points don't fight each other.

After the roof passes inspection, the array goes back on: mounts flashed into the new shingles, rails leveled, panels torqued to spec, wiring re-run and secured. The last step matters most, which is recommissioning. The crew re-energizes the system, confirms every string is producing, checks the monitoring platform, and hands you before-and-after production numbers.

That last step is the difference between a company that resets solar systems and a crew that reinstalls hardware. Roughly a third of the service calls we run are for systems another crew put back up that never got tested.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can my roofing company legally remove my solar panels in Colorado?

Generally no. The disconnect and reconnect portions are electrical work regulated by the state, and roofing companies almost never have licensed electricians on staff. Some roofers subcontract the work to real solar crews, which is legal, but you should know who's actually on your roof and confirm they're insured for solar work specifically.

### How much does it cost to have solar panels removed and reinstalled for a new roof?

Most Northern Colorado homeowners pay $2,000 to $4,000 for a full detach and reset on a typical residential system, depending on panel count, roof pitch, and racking type. Larger systems and steep or multi-level roofs run higher. If your reroof is part of a hail claim, insurance usually covers this as its own line item.

### Will removing my panels void the solar warranty?

It can if unqualified people do the work. Panel and inverter manufacturers can deny claims tied to improper handling, and many installer workmanship warranties have similar language. Using a qualified solar crew that documents the removal and reinstall protects your position if you ever need to file a warranty claim.

### How long do the panels stay off during a roof replacement?

Usually two to four days total. Panels come off one or two days before tear-off, the roof goes on, and the array is reinstalled and recommissioned right after the roof passes inspection. Weather and inspection scheduling can stretch that, but a coordinated crew keeps your production loss to a few days at most.
