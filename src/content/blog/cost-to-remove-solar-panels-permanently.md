## What Permanent Solar Panel Removal Actually Involves

"Take the panels down" sounds like one job. It's really four.

First is the panel removal itself. Each module gets disconnected, unbolted from the racking, and carried down. On a steep or two-story roof, this is slow, careful work with fall protection. Panels are heavier and more awkward than they look from the ground.

Second is the racking and mounting hardware. The rails come off, and then every mounting foot gets removed. This is the part most homeowners don't think about. Each foot is lagged into a rafter through your roofing, and every one of those penetrations has to be properly sealed or patched. On a system with 20 panels, you might have 40 or more penetrations. Sloppy patching here is how you end up with slow leaks that show up two winters later.

Third is the electrical work. The system has to be safely de-energized, the conduit and wiring removed or capped, and the inverter and disconnects taken off the wall. If you have a utility interconnection agreement, the system should be formally retired with your utility so you're not carrying a solar agreement on a house with no solar. Xcel Energy and most Northern Colorado utilities have a specific process for this.

Fourth is disposal. Panels don't go in the trash. Some can be resold or donated if they still produce, but hail-damaged or degraded panels need to go to a recycler. The [EPA has guidance on solar panel end-of-life handling](https://www.epa.gov/hw/solar-panel-recycling), and Colorado has a growing number of recycling options, but hauling and recycling fees are a real line item on your quote.

A company that quotes you a suspiciously low number is usually skipping one of these four steps. Ask which one.

## Removal vs. Detach and Reset: Don't Pay for the Wrong Job

Here's the part that surprises people. If you're removing panels because you need a new roof, permanent removal is probably the wrong service, and the wrong price.

A [solar detach and reset](/solar-detach-reset-greeley/) means we take the panels and racking down, your roofer replaces the roof, and then we put the same system back up and recommission it. You keep your solar production, your net metering agreement, and the value the system adds to your home. If you're not sure how that process works, we walk through it step by step in [what a solar detach and reset actually is](/blog/what-is-solar-detach-and-reset/).

Permanent removal makes sense in a few specific situations:

- Your panels are old or degraded and produce too little to justify reinstall labor
- Hail totaled the panels and insurance is paying for the roof but the array isn't worth replacing
- You're selling and a buyer or lender wants the system gone
- A leased system's term ended and the leasing company abandoned the equipment
- You're re-siting, going ground mount, or replacing the whole array with new equipment

If your panels still produce well and you plan to stay in the house, removing them for good usually costs you more over time than the removal itself. You lose the monthly production and you'd pay full price to go solar again later. The Department of Energy has a useful overview of [what happens to photovoltaic systems at end of life](https://www.energy.gov/eere/solar/end-life-management-solar-photovoltaics) if you're weighing whether your array is truly done.

And if hail is what started this whole conversation, get the panels inspected before you decide they're dead. Damage isn't always visible from the ground, and sometimes it's worse than it looks. ## What Drives Permanent Solar Removal Cost Up or Down

Every quote we write starts with the same handful of questions. Here's what moves the number.

**Panel count and system size.** The single biggest factor. Labor scales with panels, and so do disposal fees. A 10-panel job and a 28-panel job are different animals.

**Roof pitch and height.** A walkable single-story roof is fast. A 10/12 pitch two-story needs full fall protection, roof jacks, and more time per panel. That can add 30 to 50 percent to labor.

**Whether the roof is being replaced anyway.** This one can save you money. If your roofer is tearing off the roof right behind us, we don't need to patch every penetration to a permanent finished standard, because that decking is coming off. If the roof is staying, every hole gets sealed properly, and that's more labor.

**Inverter and electrical layout.** Microinverters mean more small components but simpler wiring. A string inverter with long conduit runs means more electrical teardown. Ground-mounted disconnects, sub-panels, and battery systems all add time.

**What happens to the panels.** Working panels that can be resold or donated sometimes offset a chunk of the disposal cost. Hail-shattered panels are pure disposal expense. Be wary of anyone who offers to "just take them to the dump" for cheap. Panels contain materials that shouldn't be landfilled, and in some cases that disposal route isn't even legal.

**Lease and loan status.** If the system is leased or has a UCC filing against it, the leasing company or lender has a say in what happens to the equipment. Sorting that out isn't expensive, but it can add weeks to the timeline if you don't start early.

One more honest note: quotes vary between companies for reasons that have nothing to do with your roof. Insurance, licensing, whether the crew includes a licensed electrician, and whether disposal is actually included all show up in the price. The cheapest quote is often the one missing something.

## How to Keep Removal Costs Down Without Cutting Corners

There are a few legitimate ways to trim the bill.

**Bundle it with your reroof.** If removal is happening because of a roof replacement anyway, scheduling the removal to land right before tear-off saves the penetration-patching labor. This is the most common savings we see, often $300 to $500.

**Ask about panel resale.** If your panels are under 10 years old and undamaged, ask whether they have resale or donation value. It won't make you rich, but it can knock the disposal line down to zero or better.

**Handle the utility paperwork promptly.** Retiring your interconnection agreement is usually free, but ignoring it can cost you later, especially at home sale time when a title company finds an active solar agreement on a house with no solar.

**Get the electrical capped by a pro, full stop.** This is the one place not to save money. A solar array is a live electrical system, and DC wiring from panels can carry lethal voltage even with the sun barely up. Homeowners can safely handle exactly none of the electrical decommissioning. If someone on a forum tells you to just pull the panels yourself and cut the wires, close the tab. The panel and racking removal itself is also genuinely dangerous work on most roofs, between the weight, the height, and the live wiring underneath.

## When to Schedule Removal and How Long It Takes

Timing matters more than most people expect, especially mid-reroof.

A typical residential removal takes one day. Larger systems, steep roofs, or complicated electrical can stretch to two. The utility paperwork runs on its own clock, usually one to three weeks, and can happen in parallel.

If a roofer is involved, coordinate the schedule so removal lands a day or two before tear-off. Panels sitting in a garage for three weeks while a roofing crew waits on materials is fine. A roofing crew standing on your driveway waiting for a solar crew that's booked out two weeks is not. In hail season around Weld and Larimer counties, both trades book up fast after a big storm, so call early.

If storm damage is forcing your hand and there's active damage under the array, don't wait on a normal schedule. A leaking roof under solar panels gets worse quietly, because the panels hide the damage from view. That's what our 24/7 line is for.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does it cost to remove solar panels permanently?

Most Northern Colorado homes fall between $1,500 and $6,000, with typical jobs around $2,000 to $3,500. The biggest factors are panel count, roof pitch and height, electrical complexity, and disposal. Get a written quote that itemizes removal, racking, penetration sealing, electrical decommissioning, and disposal so you can compare bids fairly.

### Can I remove solar panels myself to save money?

The physical unbolting looks simple, but the system stays electrically live whenever there's daylight, and DC voltage from an array can seriously hurt or kill you. You're also working at height with 40-pound panels and voiding warranties in the process. Leave the electrical work to a licensed pro at minimum, and honestly, leave the whole job to a crew with fall protection and insurance.

### Does removing solar panels damage the roof?

Done right, no. Every mounting foot leaves a penetration in the roof, and each one needs proper sealing or patching. If your roof is being replaced anyway, timing removal just before tear-off means those patches don't need to be permanent. If the roof is staying, insist on a company that documents how each penetration was sealed.

### Is it cheaper to remove panels or do a detach and reset?

Straight removal is usually somewhat cheaper up front, since there's no reinstall labor. But if your panels still produce well, removal costs you the ongoing electric bill savings and the system's value at resale. For a reroof with healthy panels, detach and reset almost always wins the long-term math. Price both before deciding.
