## Why Roof Leaks Under Solar Panels Hide for Months

A normal roof leak announces itself fast. Wind lifts a shingle, rain gets under it, and you see a stain within a storm or two.

A leak under a solar array works differently. The panels themselves shed most of the rain, so only a fraction of the water ever reaches the roof surface below them. A compromised mount or torn flashing might only take on water during wind-driven rain from a specific direction, or during a heavy spring melt when snow sits under the array for days.

That means small amounts of water can seep into your roof deck for months before enough soaks through to stain a ceiling. By the time you see the ring, the plywood around the mount may already be soft, and insulation in that bay may be wet and compressed.

This is why we tell homeowners in Greeley, Evans, and Windsor to check their attic under the array once or twice a year, especially after hail season. Ten minutes with a flashlight in October can save you a rotted deck section in April. Look for dark streaks on the underside of the roof deck, rust trails on nail tips, and any daylight around penetration points.

## What Actually Causes a Roof Leak Under Solar Panels

Almost every leak we trace under an array comes back to one of six causes. Knowing which one you're dealing with changes both the fix and the price, so it's worth understanding all of them.

**1. Lag bolts that missed the rafter.** Every roof-mounted array is anchored with lag bolts that should sink into rafters or blocking. When an installer misses the rafter, the bolt is held by nothing but plywood and sealant. The sealant fails, the bolt wallows out, and water follows the bolt straight into the attic. This is the single most common cause we find on installs that leaked within the first few years.

**2. Failed or missing flashing at the mounts.** Good installs use metal flashing that tucks under the shingle course above each mount, so water sheds over the penetration instead of into it. Fast installs sometimes skip flashing and rely on a blob of sealant. Colorado's freeze-thaw cycles and 100-degree roof deck summers destroy exposed sealant in five to eight years.

**3. Sealant that simply aged out.** Even on a well-flashed mount, sealant plays a backup role, and it has a service life. If your array is 10 or more years old and the roof underneath is original, aged sealant is a prime suspect.

**4. Hail damage around the array.** Hail bruises shingles, cracks flashing, and can fracture the sealant bond at every mount on the roof. The panels protect the shingles directly beneath them, which creates a strange pattern: the exposed roof gets replaced on the insurance scope, while damaged penetrations under the array get missed because nobody lifted a panel to look.

**5. Cracked or shifted conduit penetrations.** Your array's wiring enters the roof or wall somewhere. Those penetration points have their own flashing and sealant, and they fail the same ways mounts do.

**6. A roof that was already at end of life when the panels went on.** Panels last 25 to 30 years. If they were installed on a roof that had 8 years left, the shingles under and around the array will fail on schedule regardless of how good the solar work was. The [U.S. Department of Energy's homeowner solar guidance](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/planning-home-solar-electric-system) specifically recommends dealing with roof condition before panels go up for exactly this reason.

## Is It the Roof or the Solar Mounts? How to Tell

Before anyone quotes you a fix, you need a real diagnosis. Here's how we narrow it down, and how far you can safely get on your own.

**Start in the attic, not on the roof.** On a dry day, find the stained area and trace upward. Water travels along rafters and decking before it drips, so the entry point is usually uphill from the stain. If the trail dead-ends at a bolt tip poking through the deck, you've likely got a mount problem. If it traces to a valley, vent, or open shingle field, the roof itself is the more likely culprit.

**Pay attention to when it leaks.** A leak that only shows up during wind-driven rain from the west, or days after a snow load melts off, points to a marginal penetration that only takes on water under specific conditions. A leak with every rain points to a more open failure.

**Check the roof's age and the array's age.** A 22-year-old roof with 6-year-old panels probably has a shingle problem. A 5-year-old roof with 5-year-old panels probably has an install problem.

**Know where DIY stops.** Attic inspection with a flashlight is fair game. Walking a roof around energized solar equipment is not. The panels and their wiring carry live DC voltage whenever the sun is up, mounts and rails are trip hazards, and lifting a panel wrong can crack it or void its warranty. Once the diagnosis requires lifting panels or opening up mounts, that's the moment to call a pro. ## Fixing a Leak Under Solar Panels: Your Real Options

Once you know the cause, the fix falls into one of four buckets.

**Option 1: Sealant-only patch.** Someone climbs up, finds the suspect mount, and reseals it without lifting panels. It's the cheapest option and the one most likely to fail, because you can't properly flash a mount you can't fully access, and you usually can't even confirm you found the right mount. We consider this a stopgap to buy time before a real repair, not a fix.

**Option 2: Partial detach and repair.** We remove only the panels over the problem area, pull the failed mounts, repair the decking if needed, install new flashed mounts, and reset the panels. This is the right call when the roof itself has plenty of life left and the failure is localized. Most jobs like this take one day.

**Option 3: Full detach and reset with roof work.** If the roof is at end of life or a hail claim is paying for a replacement, the whole array comes off, the roofer does their work on a clean deck, and we reinstall on new, properly flashed mounts. If you're not familiar with how that process works, our plain-English explainer on [what a solar detach and reset actually involves](/blog/what-is-solar-detach-and-reset/) walks through it step by step. This is the gold standard fix because every penetration on the roof gets renewed at once.

**Option 4: Permanent removal.** Rare, but if the system is old, underproducing, or the numbers no longer work, some homeowners have us remove the array for good and cap the roof cleanly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do solar panels cause roof leaks?

Panels themselves don't cause leaks, but the mounting hardware penetrates your roof, and any penetration is a potential leak point if it's installed badly or ages out. A properly flashed install on a healthy roof can go decades without a drip. Most leaks we trace come from missed rafters, skipped flashing, or sealant that simply reached the end of its life.

### Can a roofer fix a leak under my panels without removing them?

Not properly. Flashing a mount requires lifting the surrounding shingles and usually the panels above it, and reputable roofers won't warranty work they can't fully access. Sealant applied around the edges of an installed mount is a temporary patch at best. A partial detach of just the affected panels is usually the honest fix.

### Will my homeowners insurance cover a leak under my solar array?

It depends on the cause. Sudden storm damage, including hail that compromised your mounts or flashing, is typically covered, and the detach and reset labor can be added to the claim scope. Slow leaks from installer error or plain old wear are usually not covered, though a newer install may still be under the installer's workmanship warranty.

### How long does a repair like this take?

A partial detach with mount repair is usually a one-day job, including resetting the panels and confirming the system is back online. A full detach and reset around a reroof typically adds one day on each side of the roofing work. Interior drywall and paint repairs, if the leak ran a while, are scheduled separately and add a few days.
