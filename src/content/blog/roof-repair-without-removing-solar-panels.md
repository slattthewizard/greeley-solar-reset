You've got a stain on the ceiling or a drip in the attic, and there's an array sitting on that roof. The first question is usually whether a roofer can just fix it, or whether the panels have to come off first. The honest answer is that it depends on where the water is getting in.

Sometimes a leak can be repaired without removing solar panels, and it takes a roofer an afternoon. Other times the panels have to come off, because the problem is underneath them and nobody can reach it. This page helps you work out which situation you're in. If you're still trying to find the source of the water, start with our guide to [diagnosing a roof leak under solar panels](/blog/roof-leak-under-solar-panels/), then come back here.

## When the repair can happen with the array in place

A roofer can usually work around the array when the leak is clearly outside its footprint. A few common cases:

- **A vent, chimney, or skylight flashing away from the panels.** These are ordinary roof leaks that happen to be on a roof with solar. The array isn't involved.
- **A valley or ridge problem on a different roof plane.** If the panels are on the south slope and the water is coming in on the north slope, it's a normal repair.
- **Failed flashing or sealant at the edge of the array.** If the roofer can see and reach the problem from the open shingles beside the panels, they can often fix it without lifting anything.
- **A few damaged shingles in the open roof between rows or beside the array.** A patch or shingle replacement in reachable areas is routine.
- **Accessible exposed fasteners or sealant at an edge row.** Sometimes the leaking spot sits right at the lowest or outermost row, where a roofer can get a hand and a tool in.

In these cases the roofer is working on bare roof, and the array is just something to stay clear of. Ask them to confirm in writing which area they're touching, so there's no argument later about who stepped where. If you're not sure a roofer should be working near your system at all, our post on [whether roofers can remove solar panels](/blog/can-roofers-remove-solar-panels/) explains where their job ends and ours starts.

## When the panels have to come off

Here's the part people hope isn't true. If the water is getting in under the array, a roofer can't fix what they can't see or reach. Panels sit a few inches above the shingles, but the racking, rails, and mounts make that space hard to work in, and the roof surface under a full row is basically sealed away from anyone with a tool.

Panels generally need to come off when:

- **The leak is under the array.** You can tell by the stain location, by water tracks in the attic that line up with the panel field, and by dry spots outside it.
- **Decking is soft or rotted.** Wood that's been wet for a long time has to be cut out and replaced, and that means opening the roof where the panels are sitting.
- **A mount flashing has failed.** Every attachment point is a hole in your roof. If the flashing at one of them has let go, the fix is at that exact spot, usually under a rail and a panel.
- **The roofer needs room to replace shingles under the field.** Weathered shingles around many mounts are hard to swap without lifting rails.

If it's a mount that's leaking, there's a related question we get all the time, which is whether the panels caused the leak in the first place. We cover that in [do solar panels cause roof leaks](/blog/do-solar-panels-cause-roof-leaks/), so we won't repeat it here.

## The middle option: detach a few panels

You don't always have to take the whole array off. Sometimes the leak sits under one or two rows, and only the panels covering that area need to move.

A partial detach means we disconnect and lift just the panels over the problem spot, store them, let the roofer make the repair, and then reset those panels on new flashed mounts. The rest of the array stays where it is and keeps working, depending on how your strings are wired.

On pricing, detach and reset generally runs $200 to $500 per panel, so pulling three or four panels for a targeted repair costs a fraction of a full-array job. We quote the actual number in writing once we've seen the layout.

There are limits. Panels are wired in strings, so lifting a few can take a whole string offline until they're back. Panels in the middle of the field can be harder to reach than panels on the edge, and the electrical work still has to be done correctly. We'll tell you if a partial detach isn't going to save you much and the whole array should come down.

If the leak is serious enough that you're already planning the reroof, it often makes more sense to detach the full array once, fix everything, and reset it new. Our page on [solar detach and reset in Greeley](/solar-detach-reset-greeley/) covers how that job goes.

## How to tell which one you've got

You can narrow it down before anyone climbs a ladder.

**Look at the timing.** A leak that shows up in the first heavy rain after a hail storm may be damaged shingles or flashing. A leak that appears slowly, over a season, often points to something that has been failing for a while.

**Check the attic.** With a flashlight, look at the underside of the decking near where the stain is. Dark tracks, damp insulation, or wet wood directly under the panel field is a strong sign the water is getting in under the array. If the wet spot is up near a vent or chimney, it's likely something else.

**Look from outside.** From the ground or a safe window, check whether shingles near the leak look cracked, lifted, or missing. Never walk a roof with solar on it. Panels are slippery when wet and the glass is easy to break.

**Ask the roofer to say it out loud.** A good roofer will tell you, before they touch anything, whether the problem is inside or outside the array footprint. If they won't commit, that's a sign they want the panels off to get a look.

## Don't let a roofer improvise around the panels

This is the part that causes the most expensive mistakes. A roofer who isn't comfortable working around solar sometimes tries to slide a sheet of flashing under a panel row, pry a rail up a little, or lean on the glass to reach a spot. That's how panels crack and wiring gets pinched.

If the repair needs panels moved, the clean way is a proper detach by someone who handles the electrical side too. If a roofer does damage a panel, you'll want to know [who pays when a roofer damages solar panels](/blog/roofer-damaged-solar-panels-who-pays/) before it happens, not after.

A small leak repaired in the open area beside the array is a one-visit job. A leak under the array is a detach, a repair, and a reset. In both cases, get the scope in writing and ask who is touching what.

## FAQ

### Can a roofer fix a leak near my solar panels without taking them off?

Yes, when the leak is outside the array footprint or at an edge the roofer can reach from open shingles. Vent, chimney, and skylight flashing, valleys on other roof planes, and damaged shingles beside the array are all normal repairs. Ask the roofer to confirm the work area in writing before they start.

### How do I know if the leak is under my solar panels?

Check the attic with a flashlight. Water tracks, damp insulation, or dark decking lined up with the panel field point to a leak under the array. A stain that sits near a vent or chimney is more likely a separate flashing issue. A roofer should be able to tell you which it is before they touch anything.

### Can you take off just a few panels instead of the whole array?

Often, yes. If the leak sits under one or two rows, we can detach only the panels over that area, let the roofer repair, then reset them on new flashed mounts. Detach and reset runs $200 to $500 per panel, so a partial job costs far less than a full array. We quote the exact figure in writing.

### Does a partial detach take the rest of my system offline?

It can. Panels are wired in strings, so lifting a few may take a whole string out of production until they're back. We tell you in advance what to expect for your layout, and the system is tested and back online after the reset.

### When is it better to remove the whole array instead?

When decking is rotted across a wide area, when several mounts are leaking, or when a reroof is already planned. In those cases one full detach and reset is cleaner than several partial ones. A detach is typically one day and a reset typically one day.
