There's a gap between your panels and your roof, and to a squirrel or a pigeon it looks like a prefab nest. Dry, shaded, sheltered from wind, and nobody bothers you up there. A solar panel critter guard is the fix, and the cheapest time to install one is when the panels are already off the roof.

If you're replacing your roof and the array is coming down anyway, this is worth five minutes before the schedule is locked.

## Why animals move in under solar panels

Panels sit a few inches above the roof surface on racking. That leaves a protected pocket that's warm in winter, shaded in summer, and out of the weather all year. Wildlife didn't have much of that on your roof before the panels went up.

Squirrels, pigeons, and other birds are the usual tenants. Nests collect leaves, sticks, feathers, and droppings. Small animals can also get at the wiring, and a roof is a long way up for a homeowner to notice any of it.

You don't need to have seen an animal for this to be a problem. Under an array, the signs show up slowly, and most people don't look under their panels until something stops working.

## What they do to the system

Here's what we see most often when we pull an array that's had visitors:

- Nesting debris packed along the lower edge and under the panels, which holds moisture against the roof surface and the hardware.
- Droppings and nest material that clog gutters and flashing areas near the array edge.
- Wiring that's been chewed, pulled loose, or stripped. Rodents chew, and the cable runs under a panel are within reach.
- Wiring that's come loose from its clips and is now dragging or resting on the roof.

Damaged insulation on the wiring is the one to worry about. Exposed conductors on a rooftop circuit are a shock and fire concern, and they can also cause a system fault that shows up as an error code, an offline monitor, or production that's lower than it should be. If your production dipped and nobody knows why, our post on [solar panels producing less after reinstall](/blog/solar-panels-producing-less-after-reinstall/) covers the common reasons, and wiring damage is one worth ruling out.

We aren't going to tell you animal damage is behind your problem without seeing it. We're saying it's one of the things worth looking for.

## What a critter guard is

A critter guard is a barrier along the open edge of the array that keeps animals from getting into the gap. It's usually a mesh or a similar screening material that's attached along the perimeter of the array, sealing the space between the panel edge and the roof.

Done properly, it lets air move and doesn't trap water or debris. It's meant to block animals without blocking airflow or drainage, and it needs to be fastened in a way that doesn't harm the panel frames or the roof.

It's not a magic shield. A guard that's loose, missing at one corner, or bent out of shape lets animals right back in, and some guards installed years ago have failed at the clips. If your array has one and you've never looked at it, that's worth a check when the panels are accessible.

## Why a detach and reset is the cheapest moment to do it

Installing or reinstalling a guard on a finished array means somebody working along the edge of a roof, reaching under panels that are bolted down. On a reroof, the panels are coming off anyway. Old nesting debris gets cleared while the roof is open, the wiring gets looked at, and the guard is fitted as the array goes back up, with the crew already on the roof and every edge in reach.

There's a cost reason too. The labor to get to the array edge is already covered by the detach and reset. Adding a guard to that visit usually means adding the guard and the time to fit it, not a separate trip. We don't have a flat price for it because the length of the array edge, the roof layout, and the guard material all vary, so we quote it with the reset.

The same logic applies if you already have guards. When the array comes off, an old guard can be repaired or replaced as part of the reinstall. When it's on the roof, you're guessing.

## What we check on the wiring when the panels are off

This is the piece most homeowners never get to see. With the array on the ground, the wiring underneath is exposed, and we look at it before anything goes back up.

During a reset, we check:

- Wire insulation along the cable runs for chew marks, cuts, or stripped sections.
- Connectors and junctions for damage, looseness, or signs of heat.
- Whether the cabling is still secured to the racking, or whether clips have broken and let it sag onto the roof.
- The racking and hardware, including any corrosion, with corroded hardware replaced.
- Nesting debris along the array edge, which gets cleared before the panels go back.

If we find damaged wiring, we'll show you and tell you what the repair involves before anything goes back on the roof. The scope and price of that depend on what's actually damaged, and we put it in writing.

Wiring checks are part of what makes a reset more than a lift and set. ## What you can do from the ground

You shouldn't climb onto the roof to check under your panels. It's a fall risk, and the wiring up there is live in daylight.

From the ground, a few things are worth watching for:

- Sticks, leaves, or feathers hanging out from the lower edge of the array.
- Birds repeatedly landing at the same spot and slipping under a panel.
- Scratching or scurrying sounds from the roof, especially at dawn and dusk.
- Droppings streaking the roof or the panel glass below the array edge.
- A drop in production, a monitoring app that's gone offline, or an inverter error with no obvious cause.

Not every one of those means an animal is in there, and the lack of them doesn't mean the space is clean. But if you see several, say so when you book, and we'll go in expecting to find some.

Keep in mind that wildlife rules apply in Colorado, and some species and nests are protected. If you find a nest, don't poke at it. Mention it when you call, and we'll deal with it the right way.

## FAQ

### What is a solar panel critter guard?

It's a barrier, usually mesh or similar screening, fitted along the edge of the array to keep squirrels, pigeons, and other animals out of the gap between the panels and the roof. It's meant to block animals while still letting air and water move. A loose or damaged one lets them back in, so fit matters.

### Can squirrels really damage solar panel wiring?

Yes. Rodents chew, and the cable runs under an array are within their reach. Chewed insulation can cause system faults, lower production, or exposed conductors, which is a safety concern. We can't say damage is behind any particular problem without looking, but wiring is one of the first things we check when panels are off.

### Why add a critter guard during a detach and reset?

Because the panels are off, the roof is open, and the whole array edge is reachable at once. Fitting a guard to a finished array means working along the roof edge under bolted-down panels. We quote the guard in writing with the reset, since the cost depends on the length of the edge and the roof layout.

### Does a critter guard cost extra?

Yes, it's an add-on to the detach and reset, not part of it. We don't use a flat number because array size, roof layout, and material all vary. We measure the edge, then give you a written quote alongside the reset so you can see both lines clearly.

### Should I go up and look under my panels myself?

No. It's a fall risk, and the wiring is live whenever the sun is on the array. Watch from the ground for debris at the array edge, birds slipping under panels, noises from the roof, or a production drop, and tell us about it when you book so we know what to expect.
