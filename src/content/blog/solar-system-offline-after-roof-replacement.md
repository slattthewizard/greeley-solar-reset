## Why Your Solar System Goes Offline After a Roof Replacement

When your panels came off for the reroof, your system didn't just get unplugged like a lamp. A proper [detach and reset in Greeley](/solar-detach-reset-greeley/) involves shutting down the whole system in a specific order: the AC disconnect, the inverter, the rapid shutdown devices, and the DC connections up on the roof. Then everything gets reversed during reinstall.

That's a lot of steps, and every step is a chance for something to get missed. Here's what usually goes wrong:

- **A breaker or disconnect was left off.** The most common cause by far. The solar breaker in your main panel, the outside AC disconnect, or the inverter's own DC switch never got flipped back on.
- **The rapid shutdown switch wasn't reset.** Code requires a rapid shutdown system that kills power at the panels. If it's still in the "off" position, your panels are fine but producing nothing.
- **The monitoring gateway lost its connection.** Enphase Envoy, SolarEdge gateway, Tesla Gateway, whatever you have. If it got unplugged during interior work or your Wi-Fi router got moved, the system may be producing power while the app shows nothing.
- **A connector didn't seat fully.** MC4 connectors on the roof click together by hand. One that's almost-but-not-quite locked can work for a day and then drop a whole string.
- **Something got damaged.** Cracked panel glass, a pinched wire under a mounting foot, a microinverter that took a knock. Less common, but it happens, especially when a roofing crew moved the panels instead of a solar crew.

## Safe Checks You Can Do Before Calling Anyone

You don't need to climb on the roof, and you shouldn't. Everything below happens at ground level with your hands off any wiring. If any of this feels unfamiliar, skip straight to the phone call.

**1. Check the solar breaker in your main panel.** Open your breaker panel and look for the breaker labeled "solar," "PV," or "inverter." If it's tripped or off, flip it fully off, then back on. If it trips again immediately, stop. Repeated tripping means a fault, and a pro needs to find it.

**2. Check the AC disconnect.** This is the gray box near your meter with a big handle. It should be in the ON position. Roofers and inspectors sometimes shut it off and forget it.

**3. Check the inverter itself.** String inverters (SolarEdge, Fronius, SMA) have a DC switch on the bottom or side. Make sure it's on. Then look at the display or lights. A green light usually means producing. Red or flashing usually means a fault code, and that code is gold. Write it down or photograph it.

**4. Give it until mid-morning.** Inverters won't wake up in the dark or in very low light. If you're checking at 7 a.m. in November, the system may just be waiting on the sun.

**5. Check the monitoring hardware.** Find your gateway box (often near the inverter or your router) and make sure it's plugged in and showing normal lights. If your Wi-Fi network name or password changed, or the router moved during construction, the gateway may need to be reconnected to your network. This is the one fix that's genuinely DIY-friendly, and most manufacturers walk you through it in the app.

**6. Wait one full sunny day after a reset.** Some systems take hours to reconnect to monitoring servers and start reporting again, so don't panic if the app doesn't update instantly.

That's the whole list. Notice what's not on it: opening the inverter, touching roof wiring, pulling panels, or "just checking" connectors. The DC side of a solar array carries lethal voltage whenever the sun is up, even with breakers off. The Department of Energy's homeowner guide on [installing and maintaining a home solar system](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/installing-and-maintaining-home-solar-electric-system) says the same thing we do: maintenance beyond basic checks belongs with qualified installers.

## Monitoring Offline vs. Not Producing: They're Different Problems

Here's a distinction that saves people real money. "Offline" in your app can mean two very different things:

**The monitoring is offline but the system is producing.** Your panels are making power, your meter is spinning backward, and only the reporting is broken. Annoying, but you're not losing a dime. The tell: check your utility meter on a sunny afternoon. If it shows power flowing to the grid, or your Xcel or Poudre Valley REA bill shows solar credits, production is happening.

**The system itself is down.** No production, no credits, and every sunny day costs you money. A typical Greeley-area home system makes roughly $3 to $6 worth of electricity per sunny summer day. Three weeks offline in July can quietly cost you $80 to $120.

## When to Stop Troubleshooting and Call a Pro

You've checked the breakers, the disconnect, and the gateway. The sun's been up for hours. Here's the bright line: if any of the following is true, stop and call a licensed solar professional.

- A breaker trips again after you reset it once
- The inverter shows a fault code, especially anything mentioning ground fault, arc fault, or isolation
- You smell anything burnt near the inverter or panel
- The fix would require touching roof wiring, connectors, or the panels themselves
- The system has been down more than a few days and nothing above worked

Ground faults and arc faults aren't "wait and see" problems. They're the inverter detecting electricity going somewhere it shouldn't, and that's a fire risk, not just a production problem. The National Renewable Energy Laboratory right up the road in Golden has published extensively on [PV system safety and performance](https://www.nrel.gov/solar/), but the short version is simple: modern inverters shut down for good reasons. Don't try to force one back on.

Timing matters too. If your reroof was part of a hail claim, your insurer expects the whole scope wrapped up, and a dead solar system can complicate the final invoice and your depreciation recovery. ## How to Keep This From Happening on Your Next Reroof

If you're reading this before the roof work starts, you're in the best position. A few habits prevent nearly every offline headache:

**Photograph your monitoring dashboard before the panels come off.** Screenshot a good production day and your system layout. That baseline settles every "was it always like this?" argument later.

**Use a solar crew for the detach and reset.** A dedicated crew labels every wire, maps every panel position, torques every connection to spec, and recommissions the system with you watching the app come back online. Roofers are great at roofs. Solar wiring is a different trade.

**Get recommissioning in writing.** Your detach and reset quote should explicitly include system startup, monitoring verification, and a production check. If a quote doesn't mention turning the system back on, ask why. It's a corner that gets cut.

**Check the app within 48 hours of reinstall.** Problems caught in the first two days are easy to pin on the responsible party. Problems discovered a month later turn into finger-pointing between the roofer, the solar crew, and you.

**Compare a full month of production once you're back online.** One weak panel out of 20 won't show up in a daily total, but it jumps out in a panel map.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should I wait before worrying about my solar system being offline after a reroof?

Give it one full sunny day after the reinstall, since inverters and monitoring gateways can take hours to reconnect and start reporting. If the app is still dark after 24 to 48 hours of decent sun, run the ground-level checks in this article. Anything beyond two or three days of no production deserves a service call, because every sunny day offline is money out of your pocket.

### Did my roofer break my solar system?

Maybe, but don't lead with the accusation. The majority of offline systems after a reroof come down to a switch, breaker, or Wi-Fi connection that nobody flipped back, not physical damage. A professional diagnostic gives you a written finding, and if the crew that moved the panels did cause damage, that document is exactly what you need to get their insurance to cover the repair.

### Is it safe to reset my solar breaker myself?

Resetting a tripped breaker once, at the panel, with dry hands, is a normal homeowner task. If it trips again right away, stop, because repeated tripping means the breaker is protecting you from a real fault somewhere in the system. Never open the inverter, touch roof wiring, or handle connectors yourself, since the DC side stays energized whenever the sun is up.

### Who pays for repairs if my system was damaged during the roof replacement?

It depends on who moved the panels and what your contracts say. If a licensed solar crew handled the detach and reset, their workmanship warranty and insurance typically cover reinstall errors. If the roofing crew moved them without solar-specific coverage, you may end up in a dispute, which is why we recommend getting the responsible party and recommissioning terms in writing before work starts. If the underlying cause was hail or storm damage, your homeowner's policy may cover it as part of the claim.
